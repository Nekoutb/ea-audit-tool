Preface

PKF International Limited administers a family of legally independent firms which carry on separate businesses under the
PKF Global name. References to PKF or PKF Global in this manual refer to the network of legally independent firms.
References to firm(s) refer to member firms of PKF International Limited.

The PKF International Audit Methodology (IAM) consists of this written manual which sets out the audit methodology or
audit approach, supplemented by a series of PKF Guidance Papers and a library of templates, available from PKF365. The
purpose of the IAM is to provide firms with a practical means of conducting audits in compliance with International
Standards on Auditing (ISAs) as issued by the International Auditing and Assurance Standards Board (IAASB).

Member firms have the option of two electronic audit platforms (CaseWare Audit International and CCH, with a PKF
approved master pack) to document their audit work. The principles and requirements of the methodology apply equally
to either of the two electronic platforms.

In addition, the PKF International Audit File contains a series of forms and templates for use in a paper-based
environment, or to supplement their audit methodology / engagement documentation (also referred to throughout as
the IAF forms). It is essential that where the IAF forms are used, they are used in conjunction with the methodology and
not in isolation. References to form numbers throughout this methodology are included for ease of use the forms.

Failure to adopt any of these options requires an annual explanation of the reasons therefore and reporting on ISA
compliance of the system in use by the member firm.

Firms who use the IAF forms must:

•  Ensure all necessary training is carried out.

•  Develop appropriate local additions as necessary for requirements of local laws and regulations. See Appendix 1 for a

list of matters which should be considered for inclusion as local additions to the audit documentation; and

•  Ensure all professional personnel involved with an audit have a comprehensive understanding of the International

Standards on Auditing (ISAs) as appropriate for their level.

The IAM is entirely consistent with the audit policies contained in the PKF International Professional Standards Manual
(IPSM) which must be adhered to by all members in the conduct of their practices.

Whilst the methodology, software platforms and the forms and templates in the International Audit File have been
designed to provide firms with a means of complying with ISAs, it does not necessarily force or guarantee compliance. It
is the responsibility of the engagement partner and team to ensure they are familiar with the contents of the ISAs and to
ensure that the work done is sufficient and appropriate in the context of the engagement and is documented to
demonstrate compliance with applicable standards. PKF International Limited accepts no responsibility for misuse or
non-compliance. In the case of an entity in a specialised industry the forms are likely to require adaptation.

This methodology will continue to be updated for changes to the ISAs and it is the responsibility of each firm to ensure
they utilise the most recent version.

The methodology and IAF forms have been updated for all ISAs effective for audits of financial statements for periods
beginning on or after December 15, 2022. For guidance on audit conclusions and reporting refer directly to ISA 700
(Revised), Forming an Opinion and Reporting on Financial Statements through to ISA 720 The Auditor’s Responsibilities
Relating to Other Information in Documents Containing Audited Financial Statements and to relevant local laws
and regulations.

100 Background and
Application of the IAM

100.01  Assumed knowledge of the International Standards on Auditing (ISAs)

The IAM is intended as a practical means to enable member firms to achieve compliance with the ISAs. It therefore
assumes a full knowledge and understanding of the ISAs, including the objectives, the requirements, the application
guidance, and the other explanatory material. The full suite of ISAs can be obtained free of charge from the publications
/ bookstore department on www.ifac.org.

All professional personnel involved with an audit must have a comprehensive understanding of the ISAs as appropriate
for their level.

100.02  ISA 200 Overall Objectives of the Independent Auditor and the Conduct of an
Audit in Accordance with International Standards on Auditing

ISA 200, Overall Objectives of the Independent Auditor and the Conduct of an Audit in Accordance with International
Standards on Auditing, provides assistance in understanding the purpose and scope of an audit. This ISA sets out how the
objectives, requirements, and guidance in all ISAs are to be understood. It is therefore key to have a clear understanding
of this ISA. For that reason, the objectives and the requirements of this ISA are replicated below:

Objectives

14.  In conducting an audit of financial statements, the overall objectives of the auditor are:

a.  To obtain reasonable assurance about whether the financial statements are free from material misstatement,
whether due to fraud or error, thereby enabling the auditor to express an opinion on whether the financial
statements are prepared, in all material respects, in accordance with an applicable financial reporting framework;
and

b.  To report on the financial statements, and communicate as required by the ISAs, in accordance with the

auditor’s findings.

15.  In all cases when reasonable assurance cannot be obtained and a qualified opinion in the auditor’s report is

insufficient in the circumstances for purposes of reporting to the intended users of the financial statements, the ISAs
requires that the auditor disclaim an opinion or withdraws (or resign) from the engagement, where withdrawal is
possible under applicable law or regulation.

Requirements

Ethical Requirements Relating to an Audit of Financial Statements

16.  The auditor shall comply with relevant ethical requirements, including those pertaining to independence, relating to

financial statement audit engagements.

Professional Skepticism

17.  The auditor shall plan and perform an audit with professional skepticism recognising that circumstances may exist

that cause the financial statements to be materially misstated.

Professional Judgment

18.  The auditor shall exercise professional judgment in planning and performing an audit of financial statements.

Sufficient Appropriate Audit Evidence and Audit Risk

19.  To obtain reasonable assurance, the auditor shall obtain sufficient, appropriate audit evidence to reduce audit risk to

an acceptably low level and thereby enable the auditor to draw reasonable conclusions on which to base the
auditor’s opinion.

Conduct of an Audit in Accordance with ISAs

Complying with ISAs Relevant to the Audit

20.  The auditor shall comply with all ISAs relevant to the audit. An ISA is relevant to the audit when the ISA is in effect

and the circumstances addressed by the ISA exist.

21.  The auditor shall have an understanding of the entire text of an ISA, including its application and other explanatory

material, to understand its objectives and to apply its requirements properly.

22.  The auditor shall not represent compliance with ISAs in the auditor’s report unless the auditor has complied with the

requirements of this ISA and all other ISAs relevant to the audit.

Objectives Stated in Individual ISAs

23.  To achieve the overall objectives of the auditor, the auditor shall use the objectives stated in relevant ISAs in planning

and performing the audit, having regard to the interrelationships among the ISAs, to:

a.  Determine whether any audit procedures in addition to those required by the ISAs are necessary in pursuance of

the objectives stated in the ISAs; and

b.  Evaluate whether sufficient, appropriate audit evidence has been obtained.

Complying with Relevant Requirements

24.  Subject to paragraph 23, above, the auditor shall comply with each requirement of an ISA unless, in the

circumstances of the audit:

a.  The entire ISA is not relevant; or

b.  The requirement is not relevant because it is conditional, and the condition does not exist.

25.  In exceptional circumstances, the auditor may judge it necessary to depart from a relevant requirement in an ISA. In
such circumstances, the auditor shall perform alternative audit procedures to achieve the aim of that requirement.
The need for the auditor to depart from a relevant requirement is expected to arise only where the requirement is for
a specific procedure to be performed and, in the specific circumstances of the audit, that procedure would be
ineffective in achieving the aim of the requirement.

Failure to Achieve an Objective

26.  If an objective in a relevant ISA cannot be achieved, the auditor shall evaluate whether this prevents the auditor from
achieving the overall objectives of the auditor and thereby requires the auditor, in accordance with the ISAs, to
modify the auditor’s opinion or withdraw from the engagement (where withdrawal is possible under applicable law
or regulation). Failure to achieve an objective represents a significant matter requiring documentation in accordance
with ISA 230 Audit Documentation paragraph 8(c).

100.04  Principles of the International Audit File (IAF) development

The forms of the International Audit File (referred to as the IAF forms) provide prompts corresponding to the
requirements as set in the ISAs. Appendix A of the International Audit File provides a mapping between the requirements
in the ISAs and the form where the matter is prompted. Note that not all ISAs are prompted directly on the forms, for
example ISA 230 Audit Documentation is not prompted.

1.  As far as possible the text in the forms is exactly aligned with the ISAs. It is intended that this will help both with the
translation of the forms and interpreting the meaning of certain terms (often additional interpretation assistance is
provided in the application guidance of a particular standard).

2.  Where considered particularly useful, smaller entity guidance as provided in the ISAs is replicated on the forms in

bold text.

3.  As far as possible the structure and format of the forms are unchanged between versions. Appendix B of the IAF

provides a brief analysis of the changes between this and previous versions of the IAF.

100.07  Electronic use of the IAF forms in Microsoft Word

The IAF forms are designed to be used electronically so that tables and pages can be expanded as necessary, i.e., work
will be recorded in the forms of the IAF in Microsoft Word as the audit progresses. If the IAF forms are used manually,
i.e., printed on paper, it is likely the printed forms will need to be supplemented with additional working papers to
ensure there is space to document the required matters.

Firms should set a clear policy as to whether it is necessary to print a complete copy of the audit file at the end of the
audit depending on the rules in their jurisdiction. However, the work is retained, the requirements in paragraph 31(f) of
ISQM1 apply, i.e., that the firm should establish policies and procedures designed to maintain the confidentiality, safe
custody, integrity, accessibility and retrievability of engagement documentation. The retention period is ordinarily no
shorter than five years from the date of the auditor’s report, although it may be longer in certain jurisdictions.

100.08  Roles and responsibilities under the IAM

The engagement partner takes overall responsibility for managing and achieving quality on the audit, including taking
responsibility for creating an environment for the engagement that emphasizes the firm’s culture and expected behavior
of engagement team members.  In doing so, the engagement partner shall be sufficiently and appropriately involved
throughout the audit engagement such that the engagement partner has the basis for determining whether the
significant judgments made, and the conclusions reached, are appropriate given the nature and circumstances of the
engagement, and that the audit report that is issued is appropriate in the circumstances.

In creating the environment described in the paragraph above, the engagement partner takes responsibility for clear,
consistent and effective actions being taken that reflect the firm’s commitment to quality and establish and
communicate the expected behavior of engagement team members, including emphasizing:

a.  That all engagement team members are responsible for contributing to the management and achievement of   quality

at the engagement level;

b.  The importance of professional ethics, values and attitudes to the members of the engagement team;

c.  The importance of open and robust communication within the engagement team, and supporting the ability of

engagement team members to raise concerns without fear of reprisal; and

d.  The importance of each engagement team members exercising professional skepticism throughout the

audit engagement.

Where the engagement partner assigns the design or performance of procedures, tasks or actions to other members of
the engagement team, the engagement partner continues to take overall responsibility for managing and achieving
quality on the audit engagement through direction, supervision and review of the work performed. The engagement
partner also takes responsibility for ensuring that the engagement team consult on difficult or contentious matters,
matters on which the firm’s policies or procedures require consultation or on other matters that, in the engagement
partner’s professional judgment, require consultation.

As indicated above, the engagement partner is required to take overall responsibility for the planning of an audit at an
early stage, by ensuring an appropriate team discussion takes place and ensuring the risk assessment is a sound basis for
the development of the work programs. There is no requirement for the engagement partner to approve the design of
the detailed work programs - rather this is the responsibility of the engagement team manager or equivalent.

The extent to which the engagement partner wishes to get involved in the planning or review of the work, e.g., by setting
out detailed instructions for the engagement manager or equivalent or by reviewing working papers in detail, is largely a
matter of professional judgment. It is expected that engagement partner will always review areas of significant risk and
significant auditor’s judgement and any other areas that they feel necessary in order to ensure that their involvement
has been sufficient and appropriate throughout the audit engagement and in order to form a basis for determining that
the significant judgments made and the conclusions reached are appropriate given the nature and circumstance of
the engagement.

The engagement manager or equivalent is the person responsible for planning the engagement and reviewing the work
to ensure it has been carried out in accordance with the plan. The engagement manager or equivalent is responsible for
taking the firm’s combined understanding of the entity and it’s environment, the applicable financial reporting
framework and the entity’s system of internal control, together with the risk assessment as agreed with the engagement
partner and turning that into a detailed program of work for the team to follow in order to produce an efficient and
effective audit complying with the firm’s procedures and other external requirements. The engagement manager or
equivalent will not need to write out the detailed work programs. Some, or all of this task may be carried out by other
members of the team with appropriate supervision. However, the manager will remain responsible for the plan. It is the
person’s role in the audit team that is important in determining them as the manager rather than the person’s job title.

Having determined the plan, the engagement manager or equivalent is responsible for ensuring that the plan is carried
out and this will be done by the review process. This does not mean that the engagement manager or equivalent is the
only person involved in the review process (for instance the senior may review the work of assistants in low-risk areas).
However, the engagement manager or equivalent retains the overall responsibility for review of the whole engagement.
The engagement manager or equivalent therefore has responsibility for the development and execution of the plan and
may well take responsibility for the project management elements of an audit such as the timetable.

The senior and the assistants are responsible for carrying out the work in accordance with the plan (as set by the
engagement manager or equivalent). There will often be several assistants involved in an engagement and they will be
allocated different tasks and responsibilities appropriate to their experience and ability.

200 The Audit Process

200.01  The objective

The objective is to carry out each audit in accordance with International Standards on Auditing (ISAs) and therefore meet
internationally accepted best professional practice, while doing so in the most efficient manner. Each stage of the audit
process is discussed in this section of the IAM.

200.02  Audit risk

Audit risk is the risk that an inappropriate audit opinion is expressed when the financial statements are materially
misstated due to fraud or error, and is a function of the risk of material misstatement of the financial statements and the
risk that the auditor will not detect such misstatement. The methodology for performing audits is designed to reduce
audit risk to an acceptably low level. As audit risk can never be reduced to zero, it is necessary to obtain sufficient,
appropriate audit evidence to be able to draw reasonable conclusions on which to base the audit opinion.

All audits should be planned and performed with an attitude of professional skepticism and professional judgement,
recognising that circumstances may exist that cause the financial statements to be misstated. In particular, an audit
should be performed recognising that a material misstatement due to fraud could exist, notwithstanding past experience
with the entity regarding the honesty and integrity of management and those charged with governance.

200.03  Reducing audit risk to an acceptably low level

In order to reduce audit risk to an acceptably low level, it is necessary to:

•  Understand all relevant aspects of the entity and its environment, the applicable financial reporting framework and

the entity’s system of internal control;

•

Identify and assess the risks of material misstatement at the overall financial statement level and at the financial
statement assertions level;

•  Determine an audit approach which responds to these assessed risks; and

•  Obtain sufficient, appropriate audit evidence to reduce audit risk to an acceptably low level.

201.01  The planning process

It is necessary to devote a considerable portion of the overall audit effort to the planning stage of the audit – evidence
suggests that audit failures generally arise from inadequate planning and risk assessment rather than poor execution.

direction to the process.  It should not, however, be assumed that procedures have to be carried out in the order
presented. Planning is a continuous and iterative process and flexibility is therefore necessary.

If any of the planning procedures in D1 Engagement Strategy Driver yield potential risks of material misstatement, they

201.05  Materiality

Early in the planning process, a materiality level for the audit must be established along with specific (lower) materiality
levels for particular classes of transactions and events, account balances or related disclosures where considered
necessary. In addition, performance materiality for the financial statements as a whole or a particular class of
transactions and events, account balance or related disclosure should be established. These should be documented on
D5.1 Materiality. Consideration as to what is material and what is not will drive many planning decisions and
determining materiality is a matter of professional judgment.

Planning the audit solely to detect individually material misstatements overlooks the fact that the aggregate of individual
immaterial misstatements may cause the financial statements to be materially misstated and leaves no margin for
possible undetected misstatements. Performance materiality (which, as explained, is one or more amounts) is set to
reduce to an appropriately low level the probability that the aggregate of uncorrected and undetected misstatements in
the financial statements exceeds materiality for the financial statements as a whole. Similarly, performance materiality
relating to a materiality level determined for a particular class of transactions and events, account balance or related
disclosures are set to reduce to an appropriately low level the probability that the aggregate of uncorrected and
undetected misstatements in that particular class of transactions and events, account balance or related disclosures
exceed the materiality level for that particular area.

Trivial refers to matters that are wholly inconsequential when judged by any criteria. Misstatements below the trivial
level (even after extrapolation or projection) need not be accumulated on B5 Summary of Misstatements. However, the
auditor must be satisfied that the clearly trivial misstatement is not an indicator of a more pervasive issue that could lead
to a potentially material misstatement.

As work progresses, materiality may need to be revised. Since work programs in the section control sheets may have
been determined on the basis of the previous materiality, it may be necessary to revise the work on the section
control sheets.

For more guidance on materiality, refer to the PKF International Guidance Paper on “Audit Materiality” available
from PKF365.

201.08  Risk assessment procedures, including analytical review

The objective of the auditor in performing risk identification and assessment procedures is to identify and assess the risks
of material misstatement, whether due to fraud or error, at the financial statement and assertion levels thereby
providing a basis for designing and implementing responses to the assessed risks of material misstatement.

ISA 315 (Revised) requires all of the risk assessment procedures within section 201.08 to 201.18 to be performed in order
to identify and assess these risks.  This is a dynamic and iterative process of gathering, updating and analyzing
information and continues throughout the audit.  Therefore, the auditor’s expectations may change as new information
is obtained.

The requirements of ISA 315 (Revised) should be applied in addition to the specific requirements of the other ISAs (e.g.,
ISA 540 for Accounting Estimates, ISA 550 for Related Parties, etc.).  Engagement teams must have a comprehensive
understanding of all of the International Standards on Auditing (ISAs) and be aware of the requirements over and above
those that are required by ISA 315 (Revised).

Risk Assessment Procedures

Risk assessment procedures shall include the following:

a.  inquiries of management and other appropriate individuals within the entity, including individuals within the internal

audit function (if the function exists);

b.  analytical procedures; and

c.  observation and inspection.

As part of the auditor’s procedures to identify risks of material misstatement at the financial statement and assertion
levels, the auditor obtains an understanding of the entity and its environment, the applicable financial reporting
framework and the entity’s system of internal control.  These aspects of the auditor’s risk identification process are
described in more detail below.

Why Analytical Procedures Are Performed as a Risk Assessment Procedure

Analytical procedures help identify inconsistencies, unusual transactions or events, and amounts, ratios, and trends that
indicate matters that may have audit implications. Unusual or unexpected relationships that are identified may assist the
auditor in identifying risks of material misstatement, especially risks of material misstatement due to fraud.

Analytical procedures performed as risk assessment procedures may therefore assist in identifying and assessing the risks
of material misstatement by identifying aspects of the entity of which the auditor was unaware or understanding how
inherent risk factors, such as change, affect susceptibility of assertions to misstatement. Types of Analytical Procedures

Analytical procedures performed as risk assessment procedures may:

•

Include both financial and non-financial information, for example, the relationship between sales and square footage
of selling space or volume of goods sold (non-financial).

•  Use data aggregated at a high level. Accordingly, the results of those analytical procedures may provide a broad initial

indication about the likelihood of a material misstatement.

Example of analytical procedures

•

In the audit of many entities, including those with less complex business models and processes, and a less complex

Analytical procedures performed as risk assessment procedures are not required to be performed in accordance with the

The procedures performed during the process of identifying risks of material misstatement must include those described

The use of automated tools facilitates the analysis of large volumes of data and helps identify anomalies within the data.
Rather than a sampling approach, data analytics can enable a complete review of all transactions within a population.
The ability to analyse complete sets of client data supports the auditor to obtain an understanding of the client’s
business model and of how transactions flow through the information system. A data-driven approach to the analytical
procedures which are required when identifying risks of material misstatements can help to identify unusual variances,
trends or anomalies which may be indicative of potential risks of material misstatement. Depending on the functionality

should plan further audit procedures to address.

Auditors can select from a variety of applications, to enable a data driven approach to risk identification and assessment.
The type of application used should be considered in context of the degree of complexity and sophistication of the
client’s information systems and processes. The audit firm should also consider the availability of training and ongoing
user-support when selecting which automated tools it makes available to its engagement teams.

For a less complex audit, it may be possible to use Excel to filter and sort client data in order to help identify risks of

Control environment

An entity’s control environment includes the oversight of management and those charged with governance, and the
responsibilities of management for the entity’s culture and supporting a commitment to integrity and ethical values
among the entity’s personnel. The control environment also includes processes and structures that address the
assignment of authority and responsibilities within the entity, how the entity attracts, develops and retains competent
individuals and how individuals are held accountable for their responsibilities in the pursuit of the objectives of the
system of internal control.

The control environment provides an overall foundation for the operation of the other components of the system of
internal control. The control environment does not directly prevent, or detect and correct, misstatements. It may,
however, influence the effectiveness of controls in the other components of the system of internal control. Similarly, the
entity’s risk assessment process and its process for monitoring the system of internal control are designed to operate in a
manner that also supports the entire system of internal control. The optional form D4.5 Control Environment
Assessment sets out prompts to aid an assessment of the control environment.  The assessment of the strength of the
control environment should be supplemented by documenting the principal features of the environment and specifically
any key weaknesses. Form D4.4 Understanding the Components of the Entity’s System of Internal Control should be
used to note the impact of the assessment on the audit. The nature of the control environment may influence the
significance of other controls or their absence. For example: the active involvement of an owner / manager may mitigate
certain risks arising from a lack of segregation of duties. It may, however, increase the risk of management override of
controls. In a smaller entity, there is unlikely to be documentary evidence in relation to the control environment and
consequently the attitudes, awareness and actions of management or the owner manager are of particular importance.

The entity’s risk assessment process

The entity’s risk assessment process is an iterative process for identifying and analyzing risks to achieving the
entity’s objectives and forms the basis for how management or those charged with governance determine the risks
to be managed.

For financial reporting purposes, the entity’s risk assessment process includes how management identifies business
risks relevant to the preparation of financial statements in accordance with the entity’s applicable financial reporting
framework, estimates their significance, assesses the likelihood of their occurrence, and decides upon actions to
manage them and the results thereof. For example, the entity’s risk assessment process may address how the entity
considers the possibility of unrecorded transactions or identifies and analyzes significant estimates recorded in the
financial statements.

An evaluation of the entity’s risk assessment process may assist the auditor in understanding where the entity has
identified risks that may occur, and how the entity has responded to those risks. The evaluation of how the entity
identifies its business risks, and how it assesses and addresses those risks assists in understanding whether the risks
faced by the entity have been identified, assessed and addressed as appropriate to the nature and complexity of the
entity. This evaluation may also assist the auditor with identifying and assessing financial statement level and assertion
level risks of material misstatement.

Form D4.4 Understanding the Components of the Entity’s System of Internal Control should be used to document the
assessment of the entity’s risk assessment process.

the assertions of management in the financial statements. Management may initiate plans, programs, or actions to
address specific risks, or it may decide to assume a risk because of cost or other considerations. Risks can arise or change
due to circumstances such as the following:

•  Changes in operating environment. Changes in the regulatory, economic or operating environment can result in

•  New personnel. New personnel may have a different focus on or understanding of the entity’s system of

Information systems

The auditor is required to understand the entity’s information system and communication because understanding the
entity’s policies that define the flows of transactions and other aspects of the entity’s information processing activities
relevant to the preparation of the financial statements, and evaluating whether the component appropriately supports
the preparation of the entity’s financial statements, supports the auditor’s identification and assessment of risks of
material misstatement at the assertion level. This understanding and evaluation may also result in the identification of
risks of material misstatement at the financial statement level when the results of the auditor’s procedures are
inconsistent with expectations about the entity’s system of internal control that may have been set based on information
obtained during the engagement acceptance or continuance process.

The information system relevant to the preparation of the financial statements consists of activities and policies, and
accounting and supporting records, designed and established to:

•

Initiate, record and process entity transactions (as well as to capture, process and disclose information about events

suspense items out on a timely basis;

•  Process and account for system overrides or bypasses to controls;

•

Incorporate information from transaction processing in the general ledger (e.g., transferring of accumulated
transactions from a subsidiary ledger);

•  Capture and process information relevant to the preparation of the financial statements for events and conditions
other than transactions, such as the depreciation and amortization of assets and changes in the recoverability of
assets; and

Control Activities

The auditor’s identification and evaluation of controls in the control activities component is focused on information
processing controls, which are controls applied during the processing of information in the entity’s information system
that directly address risks to the integrity of information (i.e., the completeness, accuracy and validity of transactions and
other information). However, the auditor is not required to identify and evaluate all information processing controls
related to the entity’s policies that define the flows of transactions and other aspects of the entity’s information
processing activities for the significant classes of transactions, account balances and disclosures.

Controls that are required to be evaluated for design effectiveness, include:

i.  Controls that address a risk that is determined to be a significant risk;

ii.  Controls over journal entries, including non-standard journal entries used to record non-recurring, unusual

transactions or adjustments;

iii.  Controls for which the auditor plans to test operating effectiveness in determining the nature, timing and extent of
substantive testing, which shall include controls that address risks for which substantive procedures alone do not
provide sufficient appropriate audit evidence; and

iv.  Other controls that the auditor has identified from the risk procedures performed and that are considered

appropriate to assess (e.g., controls that address risks assessed as higher on the spectrum of inherent risk but
have not been determined to be a significant risk, controls related to reconciling detailed records to the general
ledger, etc.).

There may also be direct controls that exist in the control environment, the entity’s risk assessment process or the
entity’s process to monitor the system of internal control, which may be identified in accordance with ISA 315
paragraph 26. However, the more indirect the relationship between controls that support other controls and the
control that is being considered, the less effective that control may be in preventing, or detecting and correcting,
related misstatements.

Control activities include information processing controls and general IT controls, both of which may be manual or
automated in nature. The greater the extent of automated controls, or controls involving automated aspects, that
management uses and relies on in relation to its financial reporting, the more important it may become for the entity to
implement general IT controls that address the continued functioning of the automated aspects of information
processing controls. Controls in the control activities component may pertain to the following:

•  Authorization and approvals. An authorization affirms that a transaction is valid (i.e., it represents an actual economic
event or is within an entity’s policy). An authorization typically takes the form of an approval by a higher level of
management or of verification and a determination if the transaction is valid. For example, a supervisor approves an
expense report after reviewing whether the expenses seem reasonable and within policy. An example of an
automated approval is when an invoice unit cost is automatically compared with the related purchase order unit cost
within a pre-established tolerance level. Invoices within the tolerance level are automatically approved for payment.
Those invoices outside the tolerance level are flagged for additional investigation.

•  Reconciliations – Reconciliations compare two or more data elements. If differences are identified, action is taken to

bring the data into agreement. Reconciliations generally address the completeness or accuracy of processing
transactions.

•  Verifications – Verifications compare two or more items with each other or compare an item with a policy, and will

likely involve a follow-up action when the two items do not match or the item is not consistent with policy.
Verifications generally address the completeness, accuracy, or validity of processing transactions.

•  Physical or logical controls, including those that address security of assets against unauthorized access, acquisition,

use or disposal. Controls that encompass:

−  The physical security of assets, including adequate safeguards such as secured facilities over access to assets

and records.

−  The authorization for access to computer programs and data files (i.e., logical access).

−  The periodic counting and comparison with amounts shown on control records (for example, comparing the
results of cash, security and inventory counts with accounting records). The extent to which physical controls
intended to prevent theft of assets are relevant to the reliability of financial statement preparation depends on
circumstances such as when assets are highly susceptible to misappropriation.

•  Segregation of duties. Assigning different people the responsibilities of authorizing transactions, recording

transactions, and maintaining custody of assets. Segregation of duties is intended to reduce the opportunities to
allow any person to be in a position to both perpetrate and conceal errors or fraud in the normal course of the
person’s duties.

For example, a manager authorizing credit sales is not responsible for maintaining accounts receivable records or
handling cash receipts. If one person is able to perform all these activities the person could, for example, create a
fictitious sale that could go undetected. Similarly, salespersons should not have the ability to modify product price
files or commission rates.

Sometimes segregation is not practical, cost effective, or feasible. For example, smaller and less complex entities may
lack sufficient resources to achieve ideal segregation, and the cost of hiring additional staff may be prohibitive. In
these situations, management may institute alternative controls. In the example above, if the salesperson can modify
product price files, a detective control activity can be put in place to have personnel unrelated to the sales function
periodically review whether and under what circumstances the salesperson changed prices.

Certain controls may depend on the existence of appropriate supervisory controls established by management or those
charged with governance. For example, authorization controls may be delegated under established guidelines, such as
investment criteria set by those charged with governance; alternatively, non-routine transactions such as major
acquisitions or divestments may require specific high-level approval, including in some cases that of shareholders.

Monitoring of controls

The entity’s process to monitor the system of internal control is a continual process to evaluate the effectiveness of the
entity’s system of internal control, and to take necessary remedial actions on a timely basis. The entity’s process to
monitor the entity’s system of internal control may consist of ongoing activities, separate evaluations (conducted
periodically), or some combination of the two. Ongoing monitoring activities are often built into the normal recurring
activities of an entity and may include regular management and supervisory activities. The entity’s process will likely vary
in scope and frequency depending on the assessment of the risks by the entity.

Matters that may be relevant for the auditor to consider when understanding how the entity monitors its system of
internal control include:

•  The design of the monitoring activities, for example whether it is periodic or ongoing monitoring;

•  The performance and frequency of the monitoring activities;

•  The evaluation of the results of the monitoring activities, on a timely basis, to determine whether the controls have

been effective; and

•  How identified deficiencies have been addressed through appropriate remedial actions, including timely

communication of such deficiencies to those responsible for taking remedial action.

The auditor may also consider how the entity’s process to monitor the system of internal control addresses monitoring
information processing controls that involve the use of IT. This may include, for example:

201.20  Assessing risks of material misstatement

This phase concentrates on an assessment of the risk of material misstatements being present in the
financial statements.

The assessment builds on the understanding of the entity gained in the risk identification phases of the engagement.

For all risks of material misstatement identified, the next required step is to determine whether they exist at:

a.  The financial statement level; or

b.  The assertion level for classes of transactions, account balances and disclosures.

Where risks exist at the assertion level, it is then necessary to determine the relevant assertions and the related
significant classes of transactions, account balances and disclosures.

Assessing risks of material misstatement at the financial statement level

For identified risks of material misstatement at the financial statement level, it is not simply sufficient just to identify the
risk exists at the financial statement level. Additionally, the auditor shall assess the risks which exist at the financial
statement level as follows:

a.  Determine whether such risks affect the assessment of risks at the assertion level; and

b.  Evaluate the nature and extent of their pervasive effect on the financial statements.

Assessing risks of material misstatement at assertion level

ISA 315 (Revised) introduces required procedures for assessing inherent risk. For identified risks of material
misstatement at the assertion level, the auditor shall assess inherent risk by assessing the likelihood and magnitude of
misstatement and determining where on the spectrum of inherent risk the identified risk is assessed.

In doing do, the auditor shall take into account how, and the degree to which:

a.  Inherent risk factors affect the susceptibility of relevant assertions to misstatement; and

b.  The risks of material misstatement at the financial statement level affect the assessment of inherent risks of material

misstatement at the assertion level.

Inherent risk factors influence the auditor’s assessment of the likelihood and magnitude of misstatement for the
identified risks of misstatement at the assertion level.  Such factors may be qualitative or quantitative, and include;
complexity, subjectivity, change, uncertainty or susceptibility to misstatement due to management bias or other fraud
risk factors, insofar as they affect inherent risk.

Appendix 2 of ISA 315 (Revised) provides further explanation about inherent risk factors as well as matters that the
auditor may consider in understanding and applying the inherent risk factors in identifying and assessing the risks of
material misstatement at the assertion level.

Assessing control risk

The engagement team may aim to test the operating effectiveness of controls, as part of the planned audit approach. If
so, the team is required to assess control risk.

Where the engagement team does not plan to test the operating effectiveness of controls, the auditor’s assessment of
control risk shall be such that the assessment of the risk of material misstatement is the same as the assessment of
inherent risk.

Evaluating the audit evidence obtained from the risk assessment procedures

After having performed the initial procedures to identify and assess risks of material misstatement, the auditor is
required to “step-back” and to evaluate whether the audit evidence obtained from the risk assessment procedures
provides an appropriate basis for the identification and assessment of the risk of material misstatement.

If not, the auditor shall perform additional risk assessment procedures until audit evidence has been obtained to provide
such a basis. In identifying and assessing the risks of material misstatement, the auditor shall take into account all audit
evidence obtained from the risk assessment procedures whether corroborative or contradictory to the assertions made
by management.

The possibility of the risk of material misstatement at the level of individual financial statement assertions should not be
overlooked and Form D7.2 Risk Assessment also provides a prompt for considering the existence of risk factors at the
level of each assertion. It is important to go through the process of considering and thinking about all material assertions
and coupling this with the understanding of the entity, identify all risk factors relevant to individual assertions.

The consideration of risks, therefore covers a wide area. However, it must be emphasised, and cannot be
overemphasised, that ultimately an auditor is only interested in identifying and assessing the risks which may lead to a
material misstatement of the financial statements.

Typically, such risks may result in a material misstatement of the financial statements at one of two levels:

•  At the level of the financial statements, as a whole; and

•  At the level of specific financial statement assertions.

For instance, the risks from a weak control environment, such as a lack of management competence, are unlikely to be
confined to specific individual items, but are most likely to have a potentially pervasive effect on the accuracy of the
financial statements as a whole - a financial statement level risk. On the other hand, significant management bonus
arrangements linked to financial performance could be held to have a pervasive effect but would also most likely
impact on specific assertions where judgmental management decisions may drive reported profits (e.g. work in
progress valuation).

Whatever their nature, risk factors must be considered in order to identify the impact on the audit.  In the case of risk
factors giving rise to the possibility of material misstatement at the level of individual assertions, this means answering
the question “what can go wrong at the assertion level?”, and an assessment (Low, Medium, High) as to the magnitude
and likelihood of the impact, including the likelihood of multiple misstatements and the impact of inherent risk factors.
Such factors include complexity, subjectivity, change, uncertainty, and susceptibility to misstatements due to
management bias or fraud. Considering the relevant inherent risk factors and the likelihood and magnitude of a material
misstatement, inherent risks should be determined at the assertion level as to the impact on the audit
(Low/Medium/High).

For each identified risk, document any related controls responsive to the risk factor identified. Where related controls
have been identified, assess control risk and the impact on the risk of material misstatement. For sampling purposes,
where the engagement team does not intend to rely on controls, the assessment of control risk shall be such that the
assessment of the risk of material misstatement is the same as the assessment of inherent risk.

Once the risk assessment process is complete, the audit response to “what can go wrong” must be recorded (specific
audit steps) on the section of D7.2 Risk Assessment titled “Audit Response”.

This section of the form should be used to summarise the responses to the risks and the impact on the audit for all
assertion level risks identified. In addition, the area may be used to summarise the audit approach to be followed for
individual areas regardless of whether significant risks have been identified. For identified risks, a cross reference to the
specific procedure designed to address the risk must be included. Such procedures will be contained on a Section Control
Sheet. The rating of “magnitude and likelihood” should be reflected in the audit approach subsequently adopted (e.g.
controls reliance versus purely substantive and the extent and nature of substantive testing.)

201.21  Identifying significant risks

Significant risks require special audit consideration and are likely to exist on most audits. What qualifies as a significant
risk requires the application of the auditor’s professional judgment.

A significant risk is defined in ISA 315 (Revised) as an identified risk of material misstatement:

i.  For which the assessment of inherent risk is closer to the upper end of the spectrum of inherent risk due to the

degree to which inherent risk factors affect the combination of the likelihood of a misstatement occurring and the
magnitude of the potential misstatement should that misstatement occur; or

ii.  That is to be treated as a significant risk in accordance with the requirements of other ISAs.

Significant risks often derive from business risks that may result in a material misstatement. In considering the nature of
the risks, the following matters are considered:

•  Whether there is a risk of fraud;

•  Whether the risk is related to recent significant economic, accounting or other developments;

International Audit Methodology • Effective 15 December 2022

34

•  The complexity of transactions;

•  Whether the risk involves significant transactions with related parties;

•  The degree of subjectivity in the measurement of financial information related to the risk, especially those involving a

wide range of measurement uncertainty; or

•  Whether the risk involves significant transactions that are outside the normal course of business for the entity, or the

transaction appears unusual.

The impact of identifying a significant risk is that:

•  The audit response must be sufficient and appropriate;

•  The controls related to the risk must be evaluated (design and implementation) and this evaluation documented as
part of the D4.6 Understand the IT Environment. If there is a control which has not been included as part of the
systems documentation (for example, a control in relation to going concern), this control should be evaluated on the
face of the form D7.2 Risk Assessment in the box with the description of the control;

•

•

If the control evaluation is satisfactory and the intention is to test controls to gain assurance as part of the further
audit procedures, then further procedures to test the controls must be performed (i.e. go a stage further than simply
evaluation); and

If the control evaluation is satisfactory but the intention is not to test controls, substantive procedures can be
performed, but these procedures must not consist of analytical procedures alone.

201.22  Risks for which substantive procedures alone are not sufficient

There may also be some risks that are of such a nature that it would not be appropriate to perform substantive
procedures alone. Form D7.2 Risk Assessment requires the identification of these. Examples of such risks may occur in
circumstances where there is highly automated processing of records with little or no manual intervention and where a
significant amount of the entity’s financial information is initiated, recorded, processed, or reported electronically in an
integrated system. The controls relating to such risks must be both evaluated and tested.

201.23  Response to the risk assessment: financial statement level risks

Such risks generally give rise to a need for a general audit response rather than specific audit procedures at assertion
level. Responses may, for instance, be:

•  Emphasising the need for a higher degree of professional skepticism.

•  Changing the level/experience of staff assigned to the audit.

•  Consideration of accounting policies.

•

Incorporating additional elements of unpredictability into the audit tests.

•  Changing the determination of performance materiality,

•  Changing the plans to test the operating effectiveness of controls, and the persuasiveness of audit evidence needed
to support the planned reliance on the operating effectiveness of the controls, particularly when deficiencies in the
control environment of the entity’s monitoring activities are identified.

•  Changing nature, timing, or extent of procedures.

•  Emphasising controls reliance instead of a substantive approach.

•  Extended engagement partner or manager review.

•

Introducing an engagement quality reviewer.

201.24  Response to risk assessment - assertion level risks

Audit procedures should be responsive to the assessed risks of material misstatement. The audit response should
concentrate on the risk areas identified and it is important to ensure the impact of the risk (after taking account of
related controls where relevant) on the audit procedures is assessed and recorded on the Section Control Sheets. D7.2
Risk Assessment should also be revisited, and a carry forward reference added to indicate on which section control sheet
the risk will be addressed.

Except for risks where substantive testing alone is not sufficient (see above), the audit response to the risk may consist of
a combination of control testing and substantive testing or substantive testing alone. If the assessment of the risk
assumes the operating effectiveness of controls, then the operation of the controls must be tested.

If the risk is one for which substantive procedures alone are not sufficient, then the operation of the controls must
be tested.

For significant risks, the relevant control will already have been evaluated. If the design of the control is such that the
control is effective and the control has been implemented, then there is a choice - either test controls or opt for a
substantive approach. If the design of the control is such that the control is either not effective or has not been
implemented, this should be reflected in the risk assessment and the approach must be substantive. In cases where
controls are not tested or testing has revealed the controls are not effective, the substantive procedures should not
consist of analytical procedures alone but must include tests of detail.

Testing of controls should be documented on a separate form titled Control Activities Testing Form. This form does not
have a fixed reference numbers so that you can assign a reference as best fits its purpose (e.g. E230.1 if it is a control test
related to inventory.)

If control testing reveals controls are poor or not operating as intended, it may be necessary to amend the planned audit
approach and this should be recorded on the same forms.

201.25  Audit programs

The final step in planning is to design a program of substantive testing, both detailed tests and substantive analytical
review, and to record these on the Analytical and Other Substantive Audit Procedures Program, part of the relevant
Section Control Sheets, referencing each step to the relevant assertions within the program sheet. The program of
planned procedures should assume that planned tests of control have yielded satisfactory results where planned
controls testing has not yet been completed. If at a later stage it is found that this is not the case, substantive procedures
will likely need to be revised.

Substantive procedures should be performed in response to the risk assessment, to the extent that further audit
evidence needs to be obtained. In addition, regardless of the risk assessment, substantive procedures should be
performed for each material class of transactions and events, account balance and related disclosures.

In designing these procedures, consider:

•  The significance of any inherent risk factors;

•  The likelihood that a material misstatement will occur and the magnitude of such a misstatement;

•  The characteristics of the class of transactions and events, account balance or related disclosures involved;

•  The nature of any specific controls used by the entity and whether they are manual or automated; and

•  Whether there is an expectation that audit evidence will confirm that the entity’s controls are effective in preventing,

or detecting and correcting, material misstatements.

The nature and extent of substantive testing is a matter for the auditor’s professional judgment having taken into
account the factors referred to above, but if substantive procedures respond to significant risks they should not consist
of analytical procedures alone, unless controls were tested satisfactorily.

In the case of smaller entities, there may not be many control activities and therefore the procedures may well be
primarily substantive.

Audit programs should be designed for every material area of the financial statements including the related disclosures.
No standard audit programs for areas such as revenue, expenses, receivables and payables are provided, but there are
some suggested risks, controls and procedures in Section 300 of this manual.

201.27  Non-Compliance with Laws and Regulations

The requirements in ISA 250 (Revised), Consideration of Laws and Regulations in an Audit of Financial Statements, are
designed to assist the auditor in identifying material misstatement

of the financial statements due to non-compliance with laws and regulations. The auditor’s responsibilities are prompted
on E310 Laws and Regulations, and are distinguished in relation to two categories of laws and regulations:

•  The provisions of those laws and regulations generally recognised to have a direct effect on the determination of
material amounts and disclosures in the financial statements such as tax and pension laws and regulations. The
requirements for this understanding are prompted on D4.2 The Entity and its Environment, and the Applicable
Financial Reporting Framework; and

•  Other laws and regulations that do not have a direct effect on the determination of the amounts and disclosures in

the financial statements, but compliance with which may be fundamental to the operating aspects of the business, to
an entity’s ability to continue its business, or to avoid material penalties.

Non-compliance with laws and regulations are defined as any act of omission or commission, intentional or
unintentional, committed by a client or employer, or by Those Charged with Governance, by management or by other
individuals working for or under the direction of a client or employer which is contrary to the prevailing laws and
regulations. The requirements for this communication are prompted on C1 Communication with those charged with
Governance and Management.

In practice, this may relate to breaches of laws and regulations as diverse as fraud, corruption and bribery, money
laundering, tax payments, financial products and services, environmental protection, and public health and safety.

The auditor may have additional responsibilities under law, regulation, or relevant ethical requirements regarding an
entity’s non-compliance with laws and regulations, which may differ from or go beyond the ISA requirements. The IESBA
Code provides a clear pathway for auditors to disclose potential non-compliance situations to appropriate public
authorities in certain situations without being constrained by the ethical duty of confidentiality.

202.01  Overview of completion process

The completion process involves:

•  Ensuring that all work that should have been performed has been completed and the appropriate conclusions drawn.

•  Ensuring appropriate review processes have been carried out.

•  Ensuring that review points (including any points raised by an engagement quality reviewer) are cleared and that

there are no significant issues outstanding.

•  Ensuring that all significant judgements and assumptions made by the engagement team are documented.

•  Obtaining written confirmation of management representations as required.

•  Carrying out subsequent event reviews and ensuring that the results are reflected in the financial statements

as appropriate.

•  Completing and signing the audit report and issuing the final financial statements to the entity.

•  Ensuring that matters which have been identified as matters to report to the entity have been reported.

•  Ensuring that any other reports required have been issued.

procedures towards the end of the audit. It prompts completion of a number of other forms. It further includes an area
to record the engagement partner’s conclusions on the audit.

Review is an important part of the audit. Overall review is carried out by the engagement partner and the engagement
manager, but more senior team members are expected to supervise and review the work of their less experienced team
members. Work should be monitored throughout the audit process. Detailed reviews should take place during the
course of the audit as each section or sub-section of work is completed. This facilitates clearing of queries immediately.

Review should:

•  Take place continuously, not just at the end of the audit.

•  Stand back from the detail and take an overview, pulling together all the understanding of the entity’s affairs and the

work done.

•

Identify any problems or gaps in the work and address them.

•  Assess whether anything could be done differently or better in future (note - this is not a substitute for obtaining

sufficient, appropriate audit evidence in the current period).

•  Be a training and development tool, forming part of an ongoing process to develop people,

A record should be made of who performed the review and the date and the extent of such review.

The review process must be face-to-face, or interactive. Interactive review is where the reviewer performs the review
function together with the person who performed the work as opposed to taking the documentation and reviewing it at
home/in the office. This isolated review is likely to be less efficient, as the reviewer wastes time trying to find
information and often correcting errors. Long lists of review queries are produced, which takes time to clear.

Interactive review is more effective for the following reasons:

•  When reviewing work together with the person who performed the procedures, misunderstandings will be avoided

as the reviewer can clear them up as they arise.

•

It creates the perfect opportunity for on-the-job coaching (i.e. explain the reasons for review notes and clarify issues
while performing the review). With isolated reviews, there is little or no feedback to the team member, other than
instructions to fix certain deficiencies in the documentation. The reviewee is unlikely to learn from this experience
and may not understand why some of the review queries were raised.

300.01  Audit programs and risk

The Section Control Sheets should be used to record in detail the audit work to be carried out and to record the overall
conclusion relating to that audit section drawn from the evidence obtained.

For convenience work is planned, carried out and recorded in sections; each section related to one aspect of the business
or financial statements. However, it is important to remember that the objective of an audit is to enable the expression
of an opinion on the financial statements as a whole and not just a series of opinions on individual items.

Audit programs should be constructed to respond to the assessed risks. These risks may have been specifically identified
as part of the risk assessment or they may be more general risks associated with the preparation of any financial
statements. It should be clear from the section control sheet what procedures have been designed in order to address
risks identified in the risk assessment. The aim is to carry out the appropriate procedures in order to reduce the risk of
material misstatement to an acceptable level.

In designing further audit procedures consider the following factors:

•  The significance of the risk (generally the higher the assessment of risk the more reliable and relevant the audit

evidence must be).

•  The likelihood of material misstatement.

•  The characteristics of the item involved, including any inherent risk factors

•  The spectrum of inherent risk

•  The nature of specific controls used by the entity and in particular whether they are automated or manual.

•  Whether it will be possible or efficient to test controls to determine whether the controls are effective in preventing

or detecting and correcting material misstatements.

•  Whether it will be possible to use substantive analytical procedures to obtain the required audit evidence.

•  Whether CAATs and in particular data analysis using IDEA or similar can be used to improve the quality of the audit

evidence or the efficiency of the procedures performed.

Substantive procedures should be performed for each significant risk, as well as each material class of transactions and
events, account balance and related disclosures, regardless of whether any risk factors have been identified. Substantive
procedures include tests of detail and substantive analytical procedures. In addition, for significant risks over which audit
evidence has not been obtained from tests of controls, substantive procedures must be detailed in nature (i.e. not just
analytical review).

300.02  Assertions

Risk of misstatement in the financial statements should be considered by reference to the assertions below. All
assertions should be considered for each category of assets, liabilities or equity, class of transactions and events or other
appropriate disclosure in the financial statements. Risks will not affect all assertions equally - for example an inherent
risk factor may indicate a risk that certain assets may have been omitted from a particular balance sheet heading
(completeness) whilst at the same time there is little risk of misstatement in the valuation of those assets that have
been recorded.

An individual procedure will often provide evidence only in respect of one assertion.  When designing procedures, it is
necessary to ensure that there is an appropriate focus covering the various assertions in respect of a particular item that
reflects the risk identified. For example, where there is an identified risk of omitted items (completeness), the majority of
procedures should not be designed to provide evidence in respect of existence/occurrence.

Where risk exists and the entity has implemented control procedures in order to eliminate or mitigate the risk, it may be

carried out. When this approach is planned an evaluation of the control must be performed to ensure that:

•

•

it appears to be operating as intended; and

it is designed to mitigate or eliminate the risk.

If the evaluation is satisfactory, then the control can be tested to confirm its operation throughout the period of reliance.
Additional procedures must be designed in respect of any residual (or uncontrolled) risk.

Controls must be evaluated and tested if the risk assessment includes an expectation that controls are operating or if the
risk is one where substantive procedures alone are deemed insufficient. It is not compulsory to test operating
effectiveness of a control in respect of a significant risk. However, if a wholly substantive approach is adopted the tests

Entities should have systems to ensure that trade payables are only recorded (and paid) in respect of valid and properly
authorised transactions.

Accuracy - where purchases are subject to retrospective volume discounts or other rebates or price adjustments there is
a risk that these amounts may be incorrectly calculated.

Other payables

308.03  Common risks associated with other payables

Other payables include accruals, deferred income, payables arising from the purchase of property, plant and equipment
or investments (outside the normal operating cycle), balances with group companies (except where arising from routine
trading), claims payable, refunds, rebates, guarantee or warranty payments (except where classed as provisions) and any
other liability not covered specifically under one of the other liability headings. Items under this heading could arise from
a large variety of different transaction or events and will not necessarily be expected to be consistent from one period to
the next.

Often the transactions or events giving rise to the liabilities will fall outside the entity's normal operating cycles and may
not be subject to the same systems and controls as are applied to other transactions. In particular, these transactions
may be more susceptible than others to management override of controls.

Completeness - the entity has failed to record liabilities either due to weaknesses in systems or due to deliberate
omission. This risk may be increased because the recording of other payables may fall outside the operating cycle.
Entities should have robust systems and controls to prevent such omissions.

Obligations - payables include amounts that are not obligations of the entity. This might arise, for example from:

•  a breakdown in the entity's system for recording payment to payables

•

fraud involving the creation of fictitious payable balances

•  creation of excessive or inappropriate 'provisions' for future expenditure (contrary to IFRS or other applicable

financial reporting framework)

•

inappropriate revenue recognition resulting in excessive deferral of income.

Valuation - accruals and deferred income are included at inappropriate amounts. Errors may arise, for example, as a
result of simple miscalculation. Alternatively, they may result from the application of an inappropriate accounting policy
with regard to revenue recognition - which may in turn arise from a misunderstanding of the requirements of IFRS or
other applicable financial reporting framework (error) or a deliberate attempt to manipulate earnings (aggressive
earnings management or fraud).

Accuracy and classification - amounts that should be classified as provisions are included within payables in the
balance sheet.

Some or all of the following procedures may be appropriate where they relate to risk of material misstatement in the

•

It should be possible to derive some assurance regarding existence, completeness, accuracy and valuation of other

concerned or their auditors. However, it should be noted that confirmations from group companies or other related

transactions giving rise to them.

whether calculations of deferred income are materially correct.

applied and, and where, applicable, adequately disclosed in the financial statements.

•  Use all the knowledge gained to date, including the work performed to understand the entity, knowledge gained

from audit work in other areas, and knowledge of significant transaction and events occurring after the balance sheet
date to consider whether there may be material unrecorded liabilities.

•  Where accruals include amounts in respect of management bonuses, consider the method used to calculate such

bonuses and whether this may create pressures on management to manipulate the entity's results or balance sheet
in order to achieve certain targets. This consideration may affect the assessment of risk relating to this and other
areas.

•  Where other payables represent estimates of amounts payable in the future relating to management's plans,

intention or expectation (such as amounts set aside for repairs, maintenance, future capital expenditure, redundancy,
retraining or re-organisation) consider whether the amount actually represents an obligation of the entity at the
period end and meets the recognition criteria set out in IFRS or other applicable financial reporting framework. Also
consider whether such payables actually represent provisions and should be classified as such in the balance sheet.

317.01  Common risks associated with finance leases and hire purchases

Completeness (assets and liabilities)/Occurrence (expenses) - leases or hire purchase (HP) contracts may be
misclassified as operating rather than finance leases, leading to inappropriate accounting treatment and disclosure. This
often arises from a failure to read the lease, together with any side letters, properly.

Rights and obligations - the entity sells an encumbered asset subject to a lease as if it is unencumbered.

Valuation - amounts for assets and liabilities related to finance leases are not determined in accordance with the
applicable accounting framework.

Completeness - in addition to the regular lease payments, many leases provide for additional payments to be made.
These may include penalties for early termination, additional amounts payable at the end of the lease based on usage
(common in motor vehicle leases), compensation payments for damage to or deterioration of the leased property. There
is a risk that inadequate provision or disclosure is made for such additional liabilities.

Completeness - selling or transferring a lease will not always relieve an entity fully of its obligations. For example, when
assigning a lease to a third party the assignor will normally retain full responsibility under the lease to the lessor in the
event of default by the assignee. There is always a risk, therefore that additional liabilities could arise unexpectedly on
leases that had been disposed of sometime in the past.

−  performing specific audit procedures to obtain evidence regarding the opening balances.

320 Cash Flow Statement

320.01  Common risks associated with cash flow statements

Since the cash flow statement merely presents the information recorded in the accounting records in a different format
from the way it is presented in the balance sheet, profit and loss account and other financial statements the main risks
associated with the cash flow statement relate to accuracy and classification. Cash flow statements are rarely derived
from accounting records designed to record cash flows - rather they are almost always derived by reanalysing
information contained in the other primary financial statements. In addition, the cash flow statement is generally less
well understood as a reporting format than the other financial statements.

Because of this there are often significant risks that:

•  non-cash items may be reported as cash flows;

•  cash flows may be inappropriately classified;

•  balances that do not meet the definition of cash or cash equivalents may be treated as cash;

•  adjustments to operating profit to arrive at cash flow from operations may include non-operating cash flows;

•

the notes to the cash flow statement may fail to disclose significant non-cash transactions;

•  cash flows relating to business acquisitions and disposals may not be correctly identified and reported;

•  cash flows relating to transactions in foreign currencies may not be reported correctly; or

•

the cash flow statement and related notes may not contain all the information required by standards.

320.02  Procedures in response to risks

Some or all of the following procedures may be appropriate where they relate to risk of material misstatement in the
financial statements:

•  The cash flow statement should be prepared or checked by re-analysing the differences between the opening and
closing balance sheets. This is the only effective way of ensuring that the statement balances and reconciles to the
other primary financial statements. Unfortunately, this procedure will not, of itself, ensure that all cash flows are
correctly classified within the appropriate subheading.

•  When adjusting profit for the period to arrive at cash flow from operations, consideration should be given to whether
payables or receivables contain items that do not relate to operations (for example, receivables or payables arising
from sale or purchase of property, plant and equipment or investments, from taxation transactions or from
origination or repayment of loan balances). Such items should be excluded when calculating the adjustment to be
made to profit.

•  Consideration should be given to the existence of material non-cash transactions.

•  Where the entity has finance leases or hire purchase transactions care should be taken to ensure that cash flows are
properly reflected in the statement - for example acquisition of an asset under a finance lease or hire purchase
arrangement does not give rise to a cash flow under the heading of capital expenditure and financial investment.

Where the entity undertakes significant transactions in foreign currencies, particular care should be taken to ensure that
cash flows are properly represented in accordance with the applicable accounting framework.

321 Fraud and Management
Override of Controls

321.01  Common risks associated with fraud and management override of controls

This is an important area of the audit. Evidence has shown that fraudulent financial reporting frequently involves the
recording of fraudulent journal entries and other non-standard journal entries. Fraudulent financial reporting may also
be achieved through applying bias in determining accounting estimates (because of the judgmental nature of estimates),
or by entering into transactions that lack business rationale. As a result, it is presumed that the risk of management
override of controls is always present and there is a need to test journal entries and other adjustments for indications of
possible material misstatement due to fraud, consider accounting estimates for management bias, and to consider the
business rationale of significant transactions.

321.02  Procedures in response to risks

Some or all of the following procedures may be appropriate where they relate to risk of material misstatement in the
financial statements:

•  Agree or reconcile the financial statements with the underlying accounting records, including the trial balance.

•  Ensure all amounts and disclosures in the financial statements have been subject to audit where necessary

and appropriate.

•  Test appropriateness of journal entries and other adjustments made in the preparation of the financial statements. In

designing and performing these audit procedures:

•  Make inquiries of individuals involved in the financial reporting process about inappropriate or unusual activity

relating to the processing of journal entries and other adjustments;

•  Test journal entries and other adjustments made at the end of a reporting period; and

•  Consider the need to test journal entries and other adjustments throughout the period.

•  Review accounting estimates for biases and evaluate whether the circumstances producing the bias, if any, represent

a risk of material misstatement due to fraud. In performing this review:

•  Evaluate whether the judgments and decisions made by management in making the accounting estimates included in
the financial statements, even if they are individually reasonable, indicate a possible bias on the part of the entity’s
management that may represent a risk of material misstatement due to fraud. If so, re-evaluate the accounting
estimates taken as a whole; and

−  Perform a retrospective review of management judgments and assumptions related to significant accounting

estimates reflected in the financial statements of the prior year.

•  For significant transactions that are outside the normal course of business or otherwise appear unusual that were

identified during the audit, consider whether the business rationale (or lack thereof) of the transactions suggests that
they may have been entered into to engage in fraudulent financial reporting or to conceal misappropriation of assets
(see D5.4 Fraud Risk Assessment Procedures).

•  When misstatements have been identified, consider whether they may be indicative of fraud and if there is such an

indication, consider the implication in relation to other aspects of the audit, particularly the reliability of management
representations.

•  Document additional procedures to be performed to respond to specifically identified and assessed risks of

management override of controls, if relevant (see D7.2 Risk Assessment).

IAASB  INTERNATIONAL AUDITING AND ASSURANCE STANDARDS BOARD
August 2009

Committed to developing international standards in the public interest.

This Questions & Answers (Q&A) publication is issued by staff of the International Auditing and Assurance Standards
Board (IAASB) to highlight how the design of the International Standards on Auditing (ISAs) issued by the IAASB under the
Clarity Project enables them to be applied in a manner proportionate with the size and complexity of an entity.
Specifically, while ISAs apply to audits of entities of all sizes and complexities, this Q&A focuses on matters that are likely
to be of particular relevance to their application in the context of an audit of a small- and medium sized entity (SME).
Small and medium practices (SMPs), other auditors of SMEs, and others with responsibility for financial statement audits
may find this Q&A helpful in effectively implementing the clarified ISAs.

This publication does not amend or override the ISAs1, the texts of which alone are authoritative. Reading this Q&A is not
a substitute for reading the ISAs. The Q&A is not meant to be exhaustive and reference to the ISAs themselves should
always be made. This publication does not constitute an authoritative or official pronouncement of the IAASB.

Background

In March 2009, the IAASB completed its Clarity Project as a result of which it issued 36 ISAs under new drafting
conventions designed to enhance the overall readability and understandability of the standards. Among other
improvements, the clarified ISAs now specify more clearly the objectives of the auditor and the requirements with which
the auditor has to comply when conducting an audit under the ISAs.

It is in the public interest that users of audited financial statements have confidence that audits of entities, whether small
or large, and simple or complex, have been performed to the same high standards. In clarifying the ISAs, the IAASB paid
particular attention to that overall goal. At the same time, however, the IAASB was alert to the needs of those that audit
SMEs—an important sector of national economies and one that is particularly sensitive to standards overload.

Accordingly, the IAASB was mindful of the importance of setting requirements that would be generally applicable in
virtually all engagements. An understanding of the design and intent of the ISAs will help auditors of SMEs to apply the
ISAs in a relevant and cost-effective way.

Q1. How do the ISAs address the fact that the characteristics of an SME are significantly different from those
of a larger, more complex entity?

The auditor’s objectives are the same for audits of entities of different sizes and complexities. This, however, does not
mean that every audit will be planned and performed in exactly the same way. The ISAs recognise that the specific audit
procedures to be undertaken to achieve the auditor’s objectives and to comply with the requirements of the ISAs may
vary considerably depending on whether the entity being audited is large or small and whether it is complex or relatively
simple. The requirements of the ISAs, therefore, focus on matters that the auditor needs to address in an audit and do
not ordinarily detail the specific procedures that the auditor should perform.2

The ISAs also explain that the appropriate audit approach for designing and performing further audit procedures
depends on the auditor’s risk assessment. For example, based on the required understanding of the entity and its
environment, including its internal control and the assessed risks of material misstatement, the auditor may determine
that a combined approach using both tests of controls and substantive procedures is an effective approach in the
circumstances in responding to the assessed risks.3 In other cases, for example, in the context of an SME audit where
there are not many control activities in the SME that can be identified by the auditor, the auditor may decide that it is
efficient to perform further audit procedures that are primarily substantive procedures.4

It is also important to note that the ISAs acknowledge that the appropriate exercise of professional judgment is essential
to the proper conduct of an audit. Professional judgment is necessary, in particular, regarding decisions about the
nature, timing, and extent of audit procedures used to meet the requirements of the ISAs and gather audit evidence. 5
However, while the auditor of an SME needs to exercise professional judgment, this does not mean that the auditor can
decide not to apply a requirement of an ISA except in exceptional circumstances and provided that the auditor performs
alternative audit procedures to achieve the aim of the requirement.6

Q2. How might the work effort in an SME audit differ from that in a larger entity audit?

Often, SMEs engage in relatively simple business transactions, which mean that their audits under the ISAs will generally
be relatively straightforward.

As an illustration, consider the requirement in ISA 315 for the auditor to obtain an understanding of the entity and its
environment.7 While the audit considerations underlying this requirement will be equally relevant for both large and
small entities, the typically simpler structure and processes in an SME often mean that the auditor may obtain an
understanding of the entity and its environment quite readily and document this in a straightforward manner.

Q5. Does the auditor have to comply with all the requirements of every relevant ISA when performing an
SME audit?

Even if an ISA is relevant, not all of its requirements may be relevant in the particular circumstances of an audit. If a
requirement is conditional and the condition does not exist, it is not necessary for the auditor to comply with the
requirement.33

A few examples of requirements that need not be applied if the relevant conditions do not exist include:

•  Holding an engagement team discussion as part of the risk assessment activities if it is only a one-person team.34

•  Performing the specified substantive and other follow-up procedures if the auditor has not identified previously

unidentified or undisclosed related parties or significant related party transactions.35

•  Obtaining sufficient appropriate audit evidence to determine whether a material uncertainty exists if the auditor has

not identified any event or condition that casts doubt on the entity’s ability to continue as a going concern.36

There is a specific documentation requirement in those exceptional circumstances where the auditor judges it necessary
to depart from a specific requirement. This documentation requirement applies only to a requirement that is relevant in
the circumstances. The ISAs do not call for compliance with a requirement that is not relevant in the circumstances.37

Q6. How does audit documentation assist the auditor in an SME audit?

At the basic level, audit documentation in an SME audit assists the auditor in planning and performing the audit. It
facilitates supervision and review of the work performed by assistants, and evaluation of the audit evidence obtained
and conclusions reached before the auditor’s report is finalised. Also, by providing a record of matters of continuing
relevance that can be simply updated, documentation provides a head start to the following year’s audit.

Of particular importance, however, is that audit documentation can help to enhance the quality of the audit in terms of
the quality of the auditor’s judgments. The soundness of decisions is often higher when the auditor takes the time to
document the facts of a significant matter and the rationale for the auditor’s conclusions; the logic and clarity in thinking
are generally enhanced.

Audit documentation, therefore, assists the overall audit process while also providing a record that may assist audit
oversight authorities and others when reviewing audit files.

Q7. How do the ISAs help guide the auditor in applying the documentation requirements in an efficient and
effective manner?

Appropriate audit documentation need not be burdensome. ISAs do much to encourage the auditor to prepare
meaningful audit documentation while fostering an effective and efficient approach to it.

Firstly, the documentation requirements in the ISAs, which set out what is expected of the auditor, are designed to result
in sufficient and appropriate audit documentation of the basis for the auditor’s report and evidence that the audit was
planned and performed in accordance with the ISAs and applicable legal and regulatory requirements.38 For this purpose,
ISA 230 requires the auditor to prepare audit documentation to enable an experienced auditor, having no previous
connection with the audit, to understand specific matters. This sets the benchmark in guiding the auditor in determining
the content and extent of the audit documentation.

In this context, ISA 230 requires the documentation to include significant matters that arose during the audit and the
significant professional judgments the auditor made in reaching conclusions on those matters.39 The emphasis is on the
significant matters and significant professional judgments. The ISA explains that an important factor in determining the
form, content, and extent of audit documentation of significant matters is the extent of professional judgment exercised
in performing the work and evaluating the results.40

Secondly, the ISAs also recognise that it is unrealistic to expect every aspect of an audit to be documented.

Accordingly, ISA 230 makes clear the following:

“… it is neither necessary nor practicable for the auditor to document every matter considered, or professional judgment
made, in an audit. … it is [also] unnecessary for the auditor to document separately … compliance with matters for which
compliance is demonstrated by documents included within the audit file…. For example, the existence of an adequately
documented audit plan demonstrates that the auditor has planned the audit.”41

Thirdly, the ISAs explicitly encourage the auditor to exercise professional judgment in determining the form and extent of
documentation. They also acknowledge how the extent of audit documentation may vary depending on the
circumstances. Examples, such as the following, are included in several places in ISA 230 and other ISAs:

•  The manner in which specific requirements in ISA 315 are documented is for the auditor to determine using

professional judgment.42

•  The form, content, and extent of documentation depend on various factors, including the size and complexity of the

entity,43 and the audit methodology and technology used in the audit.

•  The documentation for the audit of a smaller entity is generally less extensive than that for the audit of a larger

entity.44   Documentation may be simple and relatively brief.45
